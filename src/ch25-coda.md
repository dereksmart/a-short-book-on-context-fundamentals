# Chapter 25 — Coda

We have now reached the end.

Twenty-four chapters ago, we began with a sentence that I have returned to at every turn:

**Drift is a context problem, not a model problem.**

That sentence was, and remains, the thesis of this book. Everything that followed was a different way of unpacking it. The mechanics of the context window. The rules files, the commands, the directed reconnaissance. The caching, the compaction, the three scopes, the skills, the memory layers. The tool tours, the agents and teams, the harnesses, the specs, the role itself.

All of it is a response to the same underlying fact: that working well with AI is not, primarily, about asking better questions. It is about shaping the environment the AI works in so that the environment does much of the asking for you. The model is the engine. The context is the road.

The engineers who build the models will continue to improve them. The windows will grow. The middle will get slightly less dim. The benchmarks will creep upward. New architectures will be proposed and, in time, shipped.

None of that will change the basic shape of the work for you and me. Our work is, and will remain, to arrange the environment well. To write short, accurate rules. To ask shaped commands. To curate rather than hoard. To measure what happens. To repair what goes wrong.

## On practice

A last thought before we part.

The habits in this book will not produce a dramatic improvement in any one AI session. You are unlikely to write a new rules file on Monday and look back on Friday to find that your week has transformed. Craft, in most forms, does not work that way. What it does instead is accumulate.

In a month, you will find that your sessions are cleaner, without quite knowing why. In three months, you will notice that the same kinds of failure have stopped happening. In six months, you will find yourself, occasionally, producing work you did not think you had time to produce. Somebody, at some point, will ask you how you do it, and you will struggle to answer. The answer is the work you have done on the road, which is not the kind of work anyone ever sees.

This is craft. It takes practice. It rewards attention. It will, if you let it, become one of the more satisfying intellectual habits you pick up in this decade — the quiet, slowly-accumulating skill of working fluently with something new, and of watching the work improve as you do.

Go well.
